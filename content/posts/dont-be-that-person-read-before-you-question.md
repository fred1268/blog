The other day, a colleague publicly dismissed a piece of work I'd done as useless and low priority, in front of the whole team. That alone speaks for itself, I won't waste another word on it. What matters here is the dismissal itself, what happened next, and how both played out. The next day, that same colleague proposed a solution, one that made it clear he hadn't understood the problem, and certainly not the solution I'd already put in place.

Two moments, back to back, that combined a striking amount of confidence, and an equal lack of understanding and tact.

## Questions are not the problem

Let me be clear about something first: I'm not arguing against questions, or against proposing alternatives. Quite the opposite. That's how good engineering happens. A team that never questions itself is a team that's quietly accumulating blind spots.

The issue isn't *whether* you question something. It's *how* and *when*.

## Do your homework first

When someone ships something, be it a design, a pull request, a ticket, a set of slides, or anything else, really, you're always free to question it. But that freedom comes with a prerequisite: you have to actually read what's in front of you first. The slides. The documentation. The ticket description. The PR itself.

Skip that step, and you run one of two risks. Either you ask a question whose answer was sitting right there in plain view, or you ask something that has nothing to do with the actual subject. Either way, you come across as not having done the work, and you waste your counterpart's time dragging them back to ground you could have covered yourself.

What's actually sad is that whoever does this is simply too closed in on themselves to even bother understanding how it lands on the other side. Some people get annoyed, others start second-guessing themselves, others just feel quietly worn down, all on top of the time it costs them.

## Understanding before criticizing

The same logic applies, with even more force, once you move from questioning to criticizing. I mean criticizing in the constructive sense: pointing out what could be better, suggesting a different path.

You can only do that meaningfully once you've taken the time to understand the solution that was actually built, its constraints, its trade-offs, what it got right and where it compromised. That's real work. It takes time, and a fair amount of humility, to set aside your first instinct and genuinely engage with someone else's reasoning before deciding it's wrong.

Skip that step, and you're exposed to the exact same consequences as before: you look uninformed, and the other person pays for it in more than just time.

## It's not just what you say

And even once you've done the reading and the understanding, there's still the matter of how you say it. You can assert your opinion, or you can offer your take. That's a choice.

Compare "We should do X" with something like "If I understand your solution correctly, you went with Y, and my first instinct would have been X. I'm probably missing something that makes Y the better call here, what am I not seeing?" Yes, the second one takes longer to say, and it might even come across as a little overly formal at first glance. But it does something the first one doesn't: it respects the person on the other side, their decisions, and the time and effort they put into their solution. It invites a conversation instead of issuing a verdict.

## Opinions are cheap, arguments aren't

Here's the thing, once you actually think it through: a critique only deserves to be taken seriously when it's built on something, when it's informed and argued. Strip that away and what you're left with is just an opinion. And opinions are the one thing nobody is running short of.

I've written before about [what happens when everyone becomes an expert](/posts/when-everyones-an-expert-the-quiet-developers-dilemma/), confidence untethered from competence, opinions substituting for understanding. What happened with my colleague wasn't anything new or specific to him. It's that same pattern, just aimed at one piece of work instead of the industry at large.

So don't be that person: read before you question, understand before you criticize.
